# Match Every Email to One Job

Marketing email splits into three families: lifecycle emails fired by a behavior (welcome, cart, replenishment, trial), campaign emails sent to a list (launch, newsletter, webinar, fundraising, story sequences), and cold outreach to people who never opted in. The evidence converges on one design rule: each email gets one trigger, one job and one CTA, and the copy formula is chosen by how aware the reader is, not by fashion. Three-step arcs (AIDA, PAS, BAB, 4Ps) compress into short emails, while story-first formulas (Hook-Story-Offer, Soap Opera) work at sequence level. Cold email runs under roughly 80 words, while marketing emails with the highest click rates sit at 100-250 words. Triggered flows beat batch campaigns by large multiples on revenue per recipient. Open rate is no longer a trustworthy test metric because Apple Mail Privacy Protection inflates it, so clicks, conversions and revenue per recipient should judge copy. Every source behind this report is a search summary, because page fetches failed. Most numbers are vendor-reported and several conflict, so this report marks each claim as strong, weak or contested and lists gaps in a dedicated section rather than filling them from memory.

**Evidence labels used throughout:** *Sourced* = stated in a note with a URL. *Weak* = single vendor, secondhand, or untraceable. *Contested* = sources conflict. *Inference* = the research team's reasoning, not a finding. Claims the notes carried without any URL are excluded or flagged as gaps.

## Eleven email types, each with one trigger, one job and a timing rule

The notes support a clean taxonomy in which timing is anchored to behavior rather than the calendar, and the single-goal framing recurs across welcome, birthday and trial sources. The one-CTA rule is generalized from those sources and is an inference for other types. The table below is the practical core for the skill. Timings are agency and vendor consensus, not tested optima; Klaviyo itself says its recommended flow delays reflect the average brand and should be A/B tested ([Klaviyo Help](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/10948996125083-How-to-optimize-your-flow-sending-frequency)).

| Type | Purpose and trigger | Structure and timing (sourced) | Evidence note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Welcome | Deliver the sign-up promise, introduce the brand, drive first purchase | DTC template of 3 emails: brand story plus 10-15% offer immediately, social proof and bestsellers at +2 days, deadline reminder at +2 days ([Top Growth Marketing](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/welcome-email-template-dtc-brands/)). Broader guidance is 3-7 emails over 5-14 days, with discounts held until email 4-5 ([TrueFuture Media](https://www.truefuturemedia.com/articles/email-automation-sequences-that-convert)) | Discount-first and story-first conflict; story can beat discount for considered purchases or thin margins ([Darkroom](https://www.darkroomagency.com/observatory/welcome-email-examples)) |
| Abandoned cart | Recover stated intent | 3 emails: reminder about 1 hour (30 minutes for high-AOV), hesitation handler at 12-24h, urgency or incentive at 24-72h ([Omnisend](https://www.omnisend.com/blog/cart-abandonment-email), [Attribuly](https://attribuly.com/blogs/abandoned-cart-email-sequences-cadence-copy/)). Build a separate checkout-abandonment flow because a default Added-to-Cart trigger misses checkout abandoners ([Attribuly](https://attribuly.com/blogs/browse-abandonment-vs-cart-abandonment-comparison/)) | More than 3-4 emails for standard products raises unsubscribes without lifting conversion (single vendor) |
| Browse abandonment | Nudge lighter intent | Starts 2-4h after the view, then +24-48h; educational tone, discounting rarely needed ([Attribuly](https://attribuly.com/blogs/browse-abandonment-vs-cart-abandonment-comparison/)) | "Top flows send 2 emails or fewer" is secondhand ([CodeCrew](https://codecrew.us/?p=2977)) |
| Post-purchase | Reassure, then build the next purchase | Order and shipping confirmation, how-to tips, review request, cross-sell, loyalty or referral ([Litmus](https://www.litmus.com/blog/post-purchase-emails)) | Referral guidance is thin; send only if a program exists ([Moosend](https://moosend.com/blog/post-purchase-email-strategies/)) |
| Review request | Social proof generation | 7-14 days after delivery with a reminder 5-7 days later; trigger on delivery, not order date ([Top Growth Marketing](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/tools/klaviyo-flow-checklist/), [Klaviyo community](https://community.klaviyo.com/questions/5287)) | Agency consensus |
| Replenishment | Timed reorder | First nudge 5-7 days before predicted run-out, follow-up 3-5 days later, small incentive only in the final email; suppress buyers who already reordered ([Repeat](https://getrepeat.io/plays/replenishment-reminder-email-flow)) | Vendor guidance |
| Win-back and sunset | Reactivate lapsed buyers, or confirm and remove disengaged contacts | Win-back triggers after the average buying cycle (Klaviyo example: 75 days), max 3 emails, short first email with top sellers and a deadline incentive. Sunset runs off engagement, asks to update preferences or unsubscribe ([Klaviyo help](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/115002775192)) | Incentive placement differs: Klaviyo puts it in email 1, DTC sources hold it late |
| Back-in-stock and price drop | Capture dormant demand | 1-3 messages for back-in-stock ([Klaviyo help](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003872251-How-to-build-a-back-in-stock-flow)); price drop shows old and new price with a urgency CTA | Omnisend 2025 data shows back-in-stock earns the most revenue per email, $9.14 ([Omnisend](https://www.omnisend.com/blog/email-marketing-benchmarks/?bbpage=1)) |
| SaaS trial and onboarding | Activate, then convert | Cadence template of 0/1/3/5/7/14 days, each email tied to an activation event; three single-problem educational emails in the middle of a 6-email trial series ([HowdyGo](https://web.howdygo.com/blog/saas-onboarding-email-examples), [Powered by Search](https://www.poweredbysearch.com/blog/customer-lifecycle-emails-b2b-saas-trials/)). Expiry emails start 3-7 days before the end, show what the user accomplished, use one button, and go only to users who saw value ([Bento](https://bentonow.com/posts/trial-expiration-email-best-practices), [Encharge](https://encharge.io/trial-expiration-email-templates/)) | The "2-5% vs 8-15% conversion" sequence lift is vendor-reported ([Sequenzy](https://sequenzy.com/blog/free-trial-expiring-email-sequence)) |
| Birthday and anniversary | Relationship moment with an offer | Collect birthday at sign-up; send around the day; offer valid about 1-4 weeks; one CTA; reminder for unredeemed. Make it feel like a gift, not the everyday discount ([Omnisend](https://www.omnisend.com/blog/birthday-emails-tips-and-best-practices/)) | Sources disagree on timing |
| Transactional with marketing value | Reassure, cut support contacts | Confirmation with items, tracking, address and support contact. Keep transactional content larger than and ahead of any promo, to protect the CAN-SPAM transactional exemption ([Vero](https://blog.getvero.com/transactional-email/)) | Not legal advice. Opens "up to 70-90%" is an undated vendor claim |

Campaign and outbound types follow different logic because they interrupt rather than respond.

**Cold outreach** is the best-documented campaign type. Gong Labs analyzed 304,174 emails and found that on cold first touches an interest CTA ("is this worth exploring?") beat asking for a specific time, and Gong's template guide puts the advantage at 2x ([Gong Labs](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/this-surprising-cold-email-cta-will-help-you-book-a-lot-more-meetings/), [Gong templates](https://www.gong.io/files/gong-template-cold-email-templates.pdf)). A specific ask performs better once interest exists. Instantly's best campaigns stay under 80 words and run 4-7 steps spaced 3-7 days apart; **58% of replies arrive on step 1 and 42% on later steps**, and follow-ups should add a new angle, never "just checking in" ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/blog/cold-email-sequence-best-practices/)). Reply benchmarks are vendor-sourced and self-selected: Instantly reports a 3.43% average in its 2026 report versus 1.6% in its 2025 report, so the years are not comparable ([Instantly 2026](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026), [Folderly](https://generate.folderly.com/blog/cold-email-statistics-2025)). Several third-party add-ons to the Gong data (a 42% reply lift, a 44% drop for time requests) could not be confirmed and are excluded.

**Product launch** sequences follow Jeff Walker's pattern of a pre-launch content phase, a time-limited open-cart window, then cart close, with each message building interest before the product is available ([Voitto summary](https://blog.voitto.com.br/resumo/en/launch), [Smart Passive Income](https://smartpassiveincome.com/session507)). No primary source gave email-by-email timing. The cart-close logic of offer reminder, FAQ and objection email, testimonial, then deadline emails is the research team's inference. The "50% of sales on the last day" claim is unsourced and excluded.

**Story sequences and daily email** divide into two practitioner traditions. Russell Brunson's Soap Opera Sequence is five emails that warm new leads through serialized story with open and closed loops, a non-pushy first CTA and a final push in email 5 ([Lean Labs](https://www.leanlabs.com/blog/using-soap-opera-email-to-tell-your-story-and-convert-leads), [Mailmodo](https://mailmodo.com/guides/soap-opera-sequence)). Andre Chaperon is the practitioner most associated with serialized storytelling, but his actual method was not retrievable ([SuperFastBusiness](https://www.superfastbusiness.com/tag/autoresponder/)); the attribution of the format is contested between the two. A documented weakness is that mid-sequence joiners lack backstory ([Everyone Hates Marketers](https://everyonehatesmarketers.com/podcast/andre-chaperon)). Ben Settle's daily email aims to entertain, educate and elevate, drawing on an idea file of personal experiences ([Copywriter Club](https://thecopywriterclub.com/ben-settle/)); "Seinfeld email" attribution to Brunson is single-source and contested ([Sequenzy](https://www.sequenzy.com/blog/seinfeld-email-sequence)).

**Newsletters** share a predictable ritual layout plus a distinct voice with built-in sponsor slots. Morning Brew uses a greeting, headline news, bulleted sections, sponsored content and a recap, per 2019 secondary reviews that may be outdated ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.nl/morning-brew-daily-business-newsletter-overview-2019-1)); Milk Road deliberately cloned the model ([beehiiv](https://www.beehiiv.com/blog/case-study-milk-road-ben-levy-shaan-puri)).

**Nurture** follows Val Geisler's "dinner party" framework: a 5-email ecommerce series whose first email does not sell and delivers the sign-up promise, sent in real time from a real person, spaced 2-4 days apart ([Ecommerce Fastlane](https://ecommercefastlane.com/how-to-turn-new-subscribers-into-paying-customers-with-val-geislers-dinner-party-strategy/), [Privy](https://www.privy.com/blog/val-geislers-dinner-party-strategy)).

**Webinar and event** sequences use an invite 2-3 weeks out, reminders at 7 days, 1 day and 1 hour, a replay within 1-3 hours, then nurture. Every reminder carries join link, date, time and time zone ([ClickMeeting](https://blog.clickmeeting.com/?p=14754), [ON24](https://www.on24.com/blog/the-best-webinar-email-templates-to-boost-attendance/)). Average show-up is 40-50%, and the claimed lift from reminders (35-40% to 55-65%) is a vendor claim ([WebinarNinja](https://webinarninja.com/blog/webinar-email-sequence/)).

**Fundraising** is volume- and calendar-driven. M+R's benchmarks show **$54 raised per 1,000 fundraising messages** and about 50 emails per subscriber per year, 31 of them fundraising ([M+R](https://mrbenchmarks.com/email)); nonprofits raise 17-34% of online revenue in December ([Climate Advocacy Lab](https://www.climateadvocacylab.org/resource/2025-mr-benchmarks-study)). Appeal anatomy was not found.

## Seven formulas and their steps, matched to email jobs

The formulas are best understood as one arc (hook, problem or desire, proof, ask) at different granularity, which is an inference from the sourced step lists. Origins are well documented for PASTOR (Ray Edwards' own podcast) and the 4Us (Michael Masterson, applied to subject lines by Robert Bly), and only partly for AIDA, where sources disagree on dates and credit E. St. Elmo Lewis with C.P. Russell coining the acronym ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._St._Elmo_Lewis), [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/marketing/aida-model)).

| Formula | Steps | Best fit (source or inference) |
|---|---|---|
| AIDA | Attention, Interest, Desire (social proof, scarcity, life change), Action | Shorter promo emails ([Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/it/resources/copwriting-formulas/)) |
| PAS | Problem (in subject or opening), Agitate, Solve | Direct response, cold email, problem-aware audiences ([MailBakery](https://mailbakery.com/blog/best-copywriting-formulas-for-email-marketing/), [Yesware](https://www.yesware.com/blog/email-copywriting/)); Copyhackers favors it ([Copyhackers](https://copyhackers.com/10x-emails)) |
| BAB | Before, After, Bridge | Case studies and customer transformation ([MailBakery](https://mailbakery.com/blog/best-copywriting-formulas-for-email-marketing/)) |
| 4Ps | Picture, Promise, Prove, Push (some sources order Promise first) | Mid-length promo ([Swipefile](https://swipefile.com/the-4-ps-of-persuasive-copywriting)) |
| PASTOR | Person/Problem/Pain, Amplify, Story/Solution/Testimony, Transformation, Offer, Response; letter labels vary by source | Long sales and launch emails, because length pre-answers objections ([Ray Edwards Show](https://rayedwards.libsyn.com/why-your-copy-fails-pastor-review)) |
| Hook-Story-Offer | Hook, relatable story of change, offer to apply it; credited to Brunson, Dotcom Secrets | Story-led nurture and broadcast ([Ship 30](https://ship30for30.com/post/the-hook-story-offer-framework-an-easy-copywriting-formula-for-beginners)) |
| Star-Story-Solution | Relatable first-person character, story, solution as natural outcome | Cold email and lead generation ([Swipefile](https://swipefile.com/star-story-solution-copywriting-formula-for-lead-generation)) |

Two further items are not body formulas. The **4Us** (Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific, Useful, scored 1-4 each) is a subject-line evaluation checklist ([Bly](https://bly.com/Pages/DMNCOL1e[1].htm)). **QUEST** (Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition) rests on a single low-quality source and should be used only as a loose sequence outline ([Skillselion](https://skillselion.com/skills/thatrebeccarae/claude-marketing/copywriting-frameworks)). The vendor selection heuristic (AIDA for immediate action, BAB for relationship building, PAS for problem-aware audiences) is untested ([Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/it/resources/copwriting-formulas/)).

**Schwartz's awareness stages decide the lead.** The five stages are Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware and Most Aware; one message across all stages fails most of them ([Selzee](https://selzee.com/eugene-schwartz-5-levels-of-awareness), [LeadGen Economy](https://www.leadgen-economy.com/blog/five-stages-awareness-lead-generation/)). The email mapping is the research team's inference: unaware and problem-aware leads get a story or PAS opening, solution-aware readers get comparison or mechanism, product-aware readers get proof and objection handling, and most-aware readers (cart abandoners, flash-sale lists) get short offer-first emails. No email-specific performance data supports the mapping. Market sophistication is a separate five-stage model whose labels differ across summaries and should be checked against the book ([Swipefile](https://swipefile.com/5-stages-of-market-sophistication/)).

**Research precedes formula.** Joanna Wiebe's process mines customer language from surveys, interviews and reviews before writing ([Wistia](https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/joanna-wiebe-conversion-copywriting)), and Gary Halbert's lesson is that the market beats clever copy ([Lilach Bullock](https://www.lilachbullock.com/gary-halbert-business-lessons/)). Formula overuse producing inbox sameness is an inference with no quantifying study. Primary texts (Breakthrough Advertising, Boron Letters, Settle's Email Players) were not read.

## The psychology: strong on narrative and social norms, shaky on open loops and loss aversion

The notes sort mechanisms by evidence quality, and the ranking changes how the skill should use them. **Narrative transportation is the best-supported story mechanism**: a meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 articles found that imaginable plot, identifiable characters and verisimilitude raise transportation, which links to changes in affect, belief and intention ([van Laer et al. 2014](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)). That backs concrete character, setting and tension in hooks and story emails. **Social norms** have field-experiment support: hotel signs citing the majority of guests beat standard appeals, and signs matching the immediate setting ("guests in this room") worked best ([AcaWiki](https://acawiki.org/A_Room_with_a_Viewpoint:_Using_Social_Norms_to_Motivate_Environmental_Conservation_in_Hotels), [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/08/080822160338.htm)). Reported rates differ by write-up, so exact numbers should not be quoted, and the study was hotels, not email. The inference is that specific, similar-peer proof beats "thousands love us."

**Open loops need reframing.** A 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks, so the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity, but it did find a tendency to resume interrupted tasks (the Ovsiankina effect) ([Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)). Loewenstein's information-gap theory says curiosity needs some prior knowledge and a perceived attainable answer ([NOVA/PBS](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/)). The practical implication, an inference, is to promise a payoff the reader can reach quickly rather than relying on lingering memory. No controlled email test of open loops was found, which matters for soap-opera sequences that depend on them.

**Loss aversion is real on average but not universal.** A meta-analysis of 607 estimates put the mean coefficient at 1.955 ([Healthcare Economist summary](https://healthcare-economist.com/2024/06/26/empirical-estimates-of-loss-aversion)), while Gal and Rucker argue it is overgeneralized and context-dependent ([Kitces](https://www.kitces.com/blog/loss-aversion-pain-loom-larger-than-gain-gal-rucker-journal-psychology/)). Loss-framed deadline copy is plausible but must be tested per audience. **Personalization** leans on the name effect, where about one-third of people noticed their own name in an unattended audio channel ([Conway, Cowan & Bunting 2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758%2FBF03196169)); that is an audio attention result and not proof that first-name subject lines lift opens. **Pratfall** vulnerability helped a competent speaker but hurt an average one in a 48-person 1966 study ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratfall_effect)), so confessions work only where competence is already established. **Fogg's B=MAP** (Motivation, Ability, Prompt) is a design heuristic without controlled email tests; its emphasis that raising ability is more reliable than raising motivation supports single, low-friction CTAs ([behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/)). Cialdini's "unity" principle is supported only by interviews and popular press ([CXL](https://conversionxl.com/cialdini-unity/)).

**Persuasion versus manipulation is a documented line.** Mathur et al. crawled about 11,000 shopping sites and found 393 countdown-timer instances, with examples where the offer persisted after expiry or the timer reset ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.07032)). The inferred test: would the claim survive the reader seeing the full facts? Real deadlines, honest anchor prices and genuine testimonials are persuasion; fabricated timers or stock, fake "Re:" threads and guilt-trip opt-outs are manipulation. Only the timer pattern was sourced. Persuasion mapped to email parts (curiosity in the subject, transportation in the hook, social proof and anchoring in the body, ability in the CTA) is craft inference, not tested by placement.

## Component craft: subject, preview, hook, body, CTA, P.S. and length

**Subject lines.** No source settles one best length. GetResponse 2024 reports the best open rate (43.38%) at 61-70 characters, while Belkins' 5.5M cold B2B emails favor 2-4 words (46%), and Twilio's Cyber Week data shows top performers at 2-4 words ([Webtonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/email-subject-lines), [Twilio](https://twilio.com/en-us/blog/ideal-email-subject-length)). The populations differ (newsletters, cold B2B, promo), so the figures should not be averaged. Mobile may show only about 25-30 characters, so front-load key words ([Hatchbuck](https://intercom.help/hatchbuck-0fe342c41fb9/en/articles/2383225-email-subject-lines)). Emoji evidence is neutral to negative on average (GetResponse's roughly 7B-email analysis found lower opens and clicks), though MailerLite shows industry variation from -1.58% in ecommerce to +6% in education ([Lebesgue](https://lebesgue.io/email-marketing/should-you-use-emojis-in-email-subject-lines), [Prospeo](https://prospeo.io/s/subject-line-length)). Personalization in subjects is **contested**: the GetResponse analysis found personalized subjects underperformed on open, click and unsubscribe, while vendor roundups claim +22-26% opens that no original could be traced for. Cart emails are the exception, where product name plus first name is associated with the highest opens ([OptimonK](https://www.optimonk.com/abandoned-cart-subject-lines)).

Curiosity versus clarity is also contested. A NextAfter nonprofit test (n=81,631) saw a vague line beat a specific one on click rate, 2.7% to 1.6% ([NextAfter](https://www.nextafter.com/?p=47660)), and an Ian Brodie test saw curiosity lift opens from 32.3% to 36.7% ([Ian Brodie](https://www.ianbrodie.com/tag/subject-lines/)). A review of about 1,000 tests found curiosity-gap lines averaged about 22% higher opens than descriptive lines but purely vague lines did about 8% worse ([Atticus Li](https://www.atticusli.com/blog/posts/email-subject-line-testing-lessons-1000-ab-tests/)), and one HubSpot-community anecdote saw a curiosity line win opens but lose conversions ([HubSpot community](https://community.hubspot.com/t5/Email-Marketing/What-s-an-A-B-test-you-ve-performed-that-surprised-you-and-why/m-p/1046225/highlight/true)). The working rule: specific curiosity beats vague curiosity, and the deciding metric is the click or sale. Verified real-world examples are few: "4 tips to improve your golf swing this afternoon" beat "Improve your golf swing" ([Dyspatch](https://dyspatch.io/?p=471)), and OptinMonster supplies one pattern each for benefit, urgency and personalization ([OptinMonster](https://optinmonster.com/101/)). The 30+ real brand examples the project wanted were not collected; illustrative patterns (curiosity, benefit, number, question, urgency, social proof, story, negative, plain "quick question", how-to, command, newsjack) are the team's own and labeled unsourced.

**Sender name.** A real person's name beat the brand in a BlaBlaCar test by over 20% on opens ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/how-smart-testing-boosts-open-rates)); other cited lifts (19%, 27%) are untraceable. "Person at Brand" held consistently is the common compromise, and brand-only suits transactional mail ([Benchmark Email](https://www.benchmarkemail.com/blog/email-sender-name-person-or-brand/)).

**Preview text.** No controlled study exists. Vendor guidance gives conflicting lengths (40-130, 40-100, 85-100 characters) and notes mobile often shows 30-55, so the key words go first, and clients pull body text if the field is empty ([MailCharts](https://www.mailcharts.com/blog/email-preheader-text), [Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/resources/email-preheader/)). Brevo's "up to 7% higher opens" has no named research. Common pairings (extend the subject, add the deadline, deliver the curiosity payoff) are convention.

**Hook and body.** Readers scan: NN/g's 2006 newsletter research found only 19% of newsletters were read thoroughly and 69% skimmed ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/e-mail-newsletters-usability/)), and its F-pattern work warns that scanning is a symptom of poorly structured content and is web-page, not email, evidence ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Mailchimp advises about an 8th-grade reading level ([Mailchimp](https://mailchimp.com/resources/email-readability/)); other readability figures are vendor claims. Practical targets (inference): 1-3 sentence paragraphs, one idea per email, front-loaded points. Hook types (story, bold claim, question, stat, benefit, pattern interrupt, personal note) are an unsourced taxonomy with no tested data.

**CTA.** One focused action is the consistent recommendation (Whirlpool saw 42% more clicks from one CTA in a single case; the circulating "371%" figure is untraceable) ([Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/three-case-studies-to-help-improve-your-email-marketing-conversion-rates/)). Button versus link is **contested**: Campaign Monitor saw a button win by 28% while AWeber found text links won after 40 tests as the novelty faded ([Campaign Monitor](https://www.campaignmonitor.com/blog/email-marketing/buttons-email-marketing-campaigns/)). First-person copy ("Start my free trial") lifted clicks 90% on a landing page, replicated at about 24%, and lost 24.95% in a payment-page test, so it needs testing ([Mail Blaze](https://mailblaze.com/thinking/email-cta-copy-first-person-second-person), [ClickZ](https://clickz.com/me-vs-you-how-pronouns-affect-click-conversion-rates/32596/)). Name the outcome rather than "Submit"; repeating the same link in long emails is acceptable ([500.co](https://500.co/blog/one-email-one-cta)). On cold email, the interest CTA beats a time request on the first touch (Gong, above). No placement study exists.

**P.S.** Sources agree on one or two sentences with a single job: restate the main benefit from a new angle (some readers jump to the bottom), add a deadline, or give a secondary action; do not repeat body copy verbatim, introduce unrelated ideas, or use it in every email ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/16909/6-awesome-email-marketing-powers-of-the-p-s.aspx), [Mailmodo](https://mailmodo.com/guides/ps-in-email)). No data and no quotes from Halbert or Kennedy were found, so P.S. readership claims are folklore.

**Length and format.** For clicks, NP Digital found 100-249 words highest at 3.84% and over 500 words below 1% ([NP Digital](https://neilpatel.com/marketing-stats/email-click-rates-by-email-length/)); Constant Contact's data points to about 20 lines ([Drip](https://drip.com/blog/ideal-email-length)). For replies, Boomerang found 75-100 words best ([MailUp](https://mailup.com/blogs/email-length/)), and cold-email studies cluster at 20-80 words (Hunter 20-39 words at 4.5% reply, Reply.io 50-70 words, [FullEnrich](https://www.fullenrich.com/content/how-long-should-a-cold-email-be)). Different outcomes, so do not mix them. Long-form sales and launch emails have no tested comparison; the argument for length is Edwards' objection-handling logic, and the goal-matched length rule (short for clicks, longer for high-consideration or story) is a hypothesis. Plain text versus HTML is **contested**: B2B reply-driven mail favors plain text (practitioner claims of 4-9x, [Databox](https://databox.com/html-vs-plain-text-emails)), HubSpot found simpler designs always won ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/plain-text-vs-html-emails-data)), but a 700-reader newsletter test favored HTML ([Campaign Creators](https://www.campaigncreators.com/blog/html-vs-plain-text-emails)). Accessibility defaults from vendor guidance are 4.5:1 text contrast, 16px body text, meaningful alt text, a plain-text part and light and dark mode testing ([Email Love](https://help.emaillove.com/plugin/getting-started/accessibility)). Mobile and client share figures conflict widely (42-62% mobile opens; Apple about 62% of tracked opens in a July 2026 figure not verified at source, [Courier](https://www.courier.com/blog/dark-mode-email-design)).

## Sequencing, deliverability, benchmarks and testing

**Sequencing rules.** The cadences in the type table share a shape: fast first email, lengthening gaps, incentive late, and an exit at the conversion event. The exit, suppression and click-branching logic is standard practice with no quantified lift in the notes (inference). A Campaign Monitor-cited comparison of $24.9M from three cart emails versus $3.8M from one has unverified methodology ([Campaign Monitor guide](https://www.campaignmonitor.com/resources/guides/email-marketing-for-e-commerce/amp/)). Send day and time are disputed: Tuesday is most often cited, time windows conflict (8-11am and 4-6pm versus a Moosend click peak at 8-9pm), and many sources aggregate old studies ([Customer.io](https://customer.io/blog/best-time-to-send-email/), [OptinMonster](https://optinmonster.com/the-best-time-to-send-emails-heres-what-studies-show/)). No rigorous frequency-versus-unsubscribe data was found.

**Deliverability.** Since February 2024, bulk senders to Gmail and Yahoo (about 5,000+ per day) need SPF, DKIM and DMARC, RFC 8058 one-click unsubscribe honored within 2 days, and spam complaints under 0.1% target and never above 0.3% ([Resend](https://www.resend.com/blog/gmail-and-yahoo-bulk-sending-requirements-for-2024), [Bird](https://bird.com/explained/deliverability/what-are-the-gmail-and-yahoo-bulk-sender-requirements)). One source notes 0.3% was not spelled out in Google's original post. Gmail moved to delays and rejections in November 2025 ([PowerDMARC](https://powerdmarc.com/gmail-enforcement-email-rejection/)), and Microsoft rejection from May 2025 comes from one source ([IRONSCALES](https://ironscales.com/blog/googles-november-2025-dmarc-crackdown-what-security-and-marketing-leaders-need-to-know)). Primary Google and Yahoo documents were not read. For copy, spam-trigger-word lists are largely a myth, since filters weigh reputation, engagement and authentication; one vendor test showed trigger-free mail still reaching only 37.3% inbox placement from a weak-reputation domain ([Suped](https://www.suped.com/knowledge/email-deliverability/technical/are-spam-trigger-word-lists-accurate-and-should-i-be-concerned-about-them)). Copy habits that plausibly raise complaints (fake "Re:", false urgency, mismatched from-name, hidden unsubscribe, over-frequency) are inference. Cold email adds a bounce target under 2% (single vendor, [Folderly](https://generate.folderly.com/blog/cold-email-statistics-2025)). Legal basics: CAN-SPAM is opt-out, forbids deceptive subjects and headers, requires a physical address and opt-out honored within 10 business days, with no B2B exemption; GDPR and CASL are opt-in regimes ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/what-is-can-spam-ht), [AllClients](https://allclients.com/blog-resources/staying-compliant-can-spam-casl-gdpr)). Penalty figures ($53,088 per email under CAN-SPAM; EUR 20M or 4% under GDPR; CAD 10M under CASL) come from vendor blogs and need checking against FTC, EU and CRTC texts.

**Benchmarks and what to measure.** Open-rate benchmarks run from about 21% to 43% depending on vendor and Apple handling, so each number must carry source, year, platform and flow-versus-campaign label.

The directional finding holds across sources: triggered flows beat batch campaigns by multiples on clicks and revenue per recipient. The older Omnisend infographic (welcome 42.2% open, 10.5% click) contradicts the 2025 table and is treated as outdated. Highly segmented campaigns earned 3x revenue per recipient in a 2.5B-email Klaviyo study, but the data is from 2017 ([Klaviyo](https://www.klaviyo.com/marketing-resources/segmentation-benchmark-report)). Rates can also rise from list pruning alone ([MailerLite](https://www.mailerlite.com/blog/email-marketing-benchmarks)).

Apple Mail Privacy Protection pre-loads tracking pixels, and Litmus advises excluding those opens from success counts ([Litmus](https://help.litmus.com/article/405-apple-mail-opens-reported-in-email-analytics)); the share of opens it drives is cited at 50-62% ([Benchmark Email](https://www.benchmarkemail.com/blog/mail-privacy-protection)). The recommended metric hierarchy (an inference from these findings) is revenue per recipient or placed-order rate, then bot-filtered unique clicks, then click-to-open with caution, with unsubscribe and complaint rates as guardrails and open rate as directional only. For A/B tests, a 2% click baseline with a 20% relative lift needs about 20,000 contacts per variant at 95% confidence, a 30% open baseline with a 10% lift needs about 3,692, and peeking early is the most common way tests are invalidated ([Webit](https://www.webit.de/en/marketing-automation/samples-ab/), [Suped](https://www.suped.com/learn/email-deliverability/how-long-should-email-ab-tests-run-and-what-statistical-significance-is-needed-for-subject-line-)). These are vendor estimates. Small lists can detect only large effects, so results should be labeled directional.

**AI-drafted copy.** A Chicago Booth field test on one wine retailer's campaign found AI and human emails performed similarly, with hybrid drafting estimated to cut cost by 75-83% ([Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/is-email-marketing-about-be-automated)). A vendor test of 50,000 emails found humans stronger on body copy and AI on subject lines and CTAs, but the AI output was unedited ([Mailercloud](https://www.mailercloud.com/e-book/ai-vs-human-email-test-research-report)). Phrasee's "+26% opens" claim has no retrievable original. The workflow evidence supports AI first draft plus human voice and claims edit.

## Gaps and weak claims the skill should not paper over

Every finding rests on search summaries, so nothing here is verified against a primary page. The most important gaps follow. **Types not researched:** promotional/sales emails, announcements and product updates, and survey or feedback emails returned no usable sources, and fundraising appeal anatomy, launch email-by-email timing, and nonprofit and newsletter benchmarks are missing. **Practitioners not reached:** Chaperon's method, Settle's story-lesson-pitch structure, Dan Kennedy, Makepeace, Carlton, and the books themselves (Breakthrough Advertising, Boron Letters, Email Players). The notes' background-knowledge mentions of FAB, ACCA, APP and Kennedy's principles are unsourced and were not used. **Psychology not sourced:** anchoring, framing, endowment, risk reversal, peak-end, and primary studies for reciprocity, commitment, authority, liking and scarcity; there is no replication assessment of Cialdini's six principles. **Craft gaps:** no real branded subject-line swipe set, no lowercase or "ugly" style tests, no hook-type, preview-text, CTA-placement, P.S. or sign-off data, and no tested long-versus-short comparison by goal. **Benchmark gaps:** Litmus State of Email, Validity, HubSpot, Campaign Monitor and Brevo tables, and per-industry Klaviyo data were not retrieved; the Apple share of opens (50-62%) and mobile share (42-62%) are unreconciled. **Compliance gaps:** FTC, EU and CRTC primary texts and the Google and Yahoo bulk-sender documents were not read, and no guidance on disclosing AI use to recipients was found. **Contested or weak claims** to carry with caveats: AIDA dates and credit; Soap Opera and Seinfeld attribution (Brunson versus Chaperon); personalization lifts; button versus link; first-person CTAs; plain text versus HTML; the two Klaviyo benchmark sets; welcome open rates ranging from 35.53% (Omnisend 2025) to 51% (Klaviyo 2025) to 68-84% (Constant Contact 2026) to 83.6% (GetResponse), all inflated to different degrees by Apple; and the 51% welcome-series revenue lift (secondhand, [Neal Schaffer](https://nealschaffer.com/welcome-email/)). Excluded as unverifiable: Zeliq's AIDA conversion statistics, the 42%/44% add-ons to Gong's data, the 50% last-day launch claim, and the "371%" single-CTA figure.

## Conclusion

The notes change the usual picture of email copywriting in three ways. First, the strongest evidence is structural rather than stylistic: trigger-anchored timing, one job per email and metric discipline are consistently supported, while many celebrated stylistic rules (best subject length, emoji, first-name personalization, button over link, P.S. power) turn out contested or unsourced. The skill should therefore present those as hypotheses to test with a named metric, not as laws. Second, the best-evidenced psychological levers are concrete stories and specific similar-peer proof, whereas open loops, loss framing and curiosity gaps are plausible but fragile. Open loops should promise a payoff the reader can actually reach, and urgency must be real.

Third, the biggest practical risk is measurement. Apple's pre-loading makes opens unreliable for roughly half the list, vendor benchmarks differ by 10+ points for reasons unrelated to copy, and segmentation or list pruning can masquerade as copy lift. A copywriter who compares results only to like-for-like platform benchmarks, judges on clicks and revenue per recipient, and discloses list state and AI assistance will be more credible than one quoting a single open-rate figure. The next research pass should target the missing primaries (Litmus, Google and Yahoo documents, FTC text), the three unresearched email types, and real swipe-file examples.
